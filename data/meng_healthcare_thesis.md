1) Market Segmentation 

The healthcare market encompasses a broad range of segments focused on enhancing patient outcomes, improving accessibility, and reducing costs through technological advancements and innovative solutions. Healthcare can be divided into a few major sectors: Biopharma, Medical Devices, Healthcare Services, and Healthcare Technology. We are most interested in Health Tech and Healthcare Services because these sectors offer capex-light solutions that are more suited to our fund size and goals. 

Health Tech encompasses a broad range of digital health solutions designed to improve patient care, enhance the efficiency of healthcare delivery, and reduce costs. This sector includes telemedicine platforms, electronic health records (EHRs), mobile health apps, wearable devices, AI-driven diagnostic tools, and data analytics platforms. The Healthcare Services sector includes companies that provide direct patient care and support services, such as hospitals, clinics, home healthcare providers, long-term care facilities, and outpatient centers. This sector also encompasses ancillary services like medical billing, healthcare staffing, and health insurance providers. 

We have refined our focus to six segments that combine population-defined and function-defined lenses: Patient as Consumer and Healthcare Infrastructure describe how the system delivers and pays for care; Clinical Decision Support describes what a product does at the point of care; and Women's Health, Health for Seniors, and Wellness describe who the product serves. This structure is intentionally kept at six categories rather than expanded further, so that our sourcing and diligence stay focused rather than diffuse. 

Healthcare Infrastructure has been broadened from our prior “Improving Clinical Workflow and Administrative Burden” framing to explicitly capture both clinical infrastructure (documentation, interoperability) and nonclinical infrastructure (billing, revenue cycle, workforce and staffing logistics). This reflects where the market itself is moving: clinical workflow and non-clinical workflow are now digital health's two largest funded categories, together capturing 42% of all sector funding.? Clinical Decision Support is separated out as its own segment because it carries a fundamentally different risk profile, touching diagnosis and treatment decisions directly, which warrants its own evidentiary bar distinct from back-office infrastructure tools. 

Healthcare Infrastructure's clinical impact is real but indirect, and we want to be explicit about that mechanism rather than leave it implied, since it is less immediately visible than a diagnosis or a treatment decision. Ambient documentation is a useful example, though the same logic applies across this segment: the tool itself does not cure a patient, but it returns minutes of face time to every visit that would otherwise go to a keyboard, and that returned time is what makes space for the physician-patient relationship that actually drives adherence, trust, and earlier disclosure of symptoms, the real mechanisms behind better clinical outcomes. Nonclinical tools carry the same logic at a population level: a scheduling platform that reduces no-show rates, or a billing and insurance tool that keeps an independent practice financially viable, is not treating any single patient, but it is preserving or expanding a population's access to care that would otherwise be lost to closure, wait times, or consolidation. We underwrite Healthcare Infrastructure on this systems-level, public-health logic: the relevant diligence question is not whether the product touches a clinical decision directly, but whether it changes capacity, time, or access at a scale large enough to move outcomes across the patient population it serves, not only for the patients it directly touches. 

Women's Health now spans four subcategories rather than two, adding Non-invasive & Patient-Collected Diagnostics and Cardiovascular, Bone & Metabolic Health alongside Reproductive & Maternal Care and Menopause & Chronic Conditions. Both additions stay within our capex-light mandate: the diagnostics we underwrite here run on contract labs and outsourced manufacturing rather than owned hardware or lab infrastructure, consistent with our broader preference for Health Tech and Healthcare Services over capital-intensive Medical Devices and Biopharma. 

Wellness is a new segment for us, encompassing mental health and longevity. Given how quickly consumer wellness trends move, and how easily “wellness” can become a marketing label rather than a health outcome, every Wellness investment must be grounded in one of the following: published clinical outcomes data, a formal clinical advisory structure, or a partnership with a health system or payer. We are not underwriting momentum; we are underwriting evidence.

2) Problem and Challenge Statement 

U.S. healthcare functions best when centered around the relationship between patients and their care providers. However, growing consolidation across the system is eroding this foundation. Independent practices are being acquired by large hospital networks, academic medical centers, and private equity firms. As a result, care is becoming more expensive, less personal, and increasingly difficult to access, especially in rural areas and communities already facing healthcare disparities. The decline in physician-owned practices has also reduced competition, making it harder for patients to find care that feels both consistent and tailored to their needs. 

This consolidation has introduced a widespread and compounding burden across the healthcare experience. For patients, it means fewer options, longer wait times, and added complexity navigating insurance, referrals, and provider networks. The result is often delayed care or surprise medical bills that create unnecessary financial strain. For clinicians, the shift has meant more time spent on administrative tasks and electronic documentation, and less time in direct patient care. This burden is compounded by a tightening workforce: staffing shortages across nursing, primary care, and behavioral health are straining the same system from the supply side. 

This imbalance contributes to high rates of burnout and job dissatisfaction. These pressures affect not only providers' well-being but also the quality of care they are able to deliver. Vulnerable populations are hit hardest: women may struggle to access essential reproductive or prenatal services, while seniors often face fragmented management of chronic conditions. When both patients and providers are strained by the same system, the result is care that is more reactive than proactive, and far less equitable or effective than it could be. 

At the national level, shifting regulatory priorities and widespread defunding place even greater stress on healthcare delivery and public health infrastructure. In recent years, federal support for key programs, including reproductive health services, community health centers, and pandemic preparedness, has been scaled back or politicized. These cuts disproportionately affect marginalized populations who rely on safety-net systems, and they weaken the country's ability to respond to emerging health threats. As public health becomes more fragmented and underfunded, healthcare systems are forced to absorb additional pressure, often without adequate resources or support. 

To address these challenges, we believe in supporting practical, scalable solutions that improve access, streamline care, and prioritize equity. We aim to support entrepreneurial ventures across women's health, senior care, healthcare infrastructure, clinical decision support, evidence-grounded wellness, and consumer-centered healthcare delivery. 

3) Point of View on Key Trends 

The financial pressures to reduce excess medical costs, government policies promoting transparency and disincentivizing consolidation of care delivery, technological advancements, and the rise of concierge care all make it more likely that the market for products and services that support independent practitioners and a consumer-centric care delivery model will continue to grow. 

Financial Pressures 

The large and increasing costs of the U.S. healthcare system, $4.5 trillion or $13,493 per capita as of 2022, and about 17.3% of GDP, put increased pressure on policymakers, payers, and taxpayers to obtain higher value for dollars spent on healthcare.? Hospital care is the largest category of healthcare spending in the U.S., accounting for 30.4% of spending, followed by physician and clinical services at 19.8%. 

From the patient perspective, the increased prevalence of high-deductible health plans (HDHPs) has increased out-of-pocket costs. HDHPs carry lower monthly premiums and higher deductibles, at least $1,600 for individuals and $3,200 for families in 2024. About 51% of privately employed Americans had high-deductible health plans in 2023.? On average, households spend about $4,393 on out-of-pocket healthcare expenditures in the form of premiums, deductibles, co-payments, and co-insurance.? These personal financial pressures have increased demand for more affordable healthcare, as well as transparency measures around price and outcomes. 

Government Policy 

Government policy is increasingly focused on promoting price transparency and challenging monopolistic practices in healthcare. Recent federal rules require hospitals to publish detailed pricing data in machine-readable formats as well as consumer-friendly lists for common services. Cities like New York have taken independent steps to mandate price transparency and created oversight bodies, reflecting a broader national movement. 

At the same time, federal and state authorities are intensifying scrutiny of healthcare mergers and acquisitions, especially those involving private equity firms. States such as California, Indiana, and Massachusetts have enacted laws requiring notice and review of private equity transactions in healthcare. These regulatory changes are creating opportunities for companies that help healthcare providers comply with transparency requirements and compete in a more regulated environment. Leading companies like Zocdoc, Turquoise Health, RubiconMD, and Gentem Health illustrate how technology-driven solutions are reshaping healthcare delivery to be more transparent, efficient, and patient-centered. 

Technology Development 

Digital health investment has returned to steady growth after its post-pandemic pullback. U.S. digital health venture funding reached $6.4 billion across 245 deals in the first half of 2025, up from $6.0 billion in H1 2024 and $6.2 billion in H1 2023.? By the third quarter of 2025, clinical workflow and non-clinical workflow had become the sector's two most-funded value propositions, together capturing 42% of all funding, with a lead of roughly $1.3 billion over every other category.? This is the market validating the same thesis behind our renamed Healthcare Infrastructure segment: the operating layer of healthcare, not any single clinical indication, is where the largest and most durable opportunities currently sit. 

AI adoption is the primary driver of this growth. Ambient documentation tools have moved from pilot to standard-of-care adoption at large health systems, and administrative AI now accounts for a substantial share of total AI healthcare investment and an even larger share of deal volume, reflecting how many small, fast-adopting deals are happening beneath the mega-rounds. Clinical decision support and diagnostic AI, spanning radiology, pathology, and point-of-care triage, represent the next-largest and fastest-growing category, and increasingly command premium valuations as investors treat foundation-model-enabled clinical tools as durable platform bets rather than point solutions. 

Consumer-facing wellness and diagnostics have also accelerated, but the market is signaling a maturing challenge worth underwriting carefully. Direct-to-consumer diagnostics has moved past its early land-grab phase; the harder problem now is the translation layer, turning biomarker and wearable data into guidance patients will actually follow and sustain.? Longevity, precision medicine, next-generation wearables, and climate-driven health categories (such as allergy and air-quality-linked conditions) are all maturing quickly, but this is precisely the kind of momentum our Wellness segment is designed to filter rather than chase: we require clinical grounding, not just consumer enthusiasm, before we underwrite a wellness thesis. 

Mental health AI and virtual reality therapy platforms continue to build a credible evidence base. Woebot, a well-studied AI therapy chatbot, has demonstrated clinical effectiveness in managing symptoms of depression and anxiety through evidence-based cognitive behavioral techniques. Similarly, OxfordVR's “gameChange” VR therapy platform has shown benefits in treating severe anxiety and agoraphobia through immersive, controlled exposure environments, with results supported by randomized clinical trials. These are the kind of evidentiary anchors we look for across our Clinical Decision Support and Wellness segments alike. 

4) Theory of Change 

An array of investments in products and services that support the independence of patients and independent clinical providers will help increase timely access to patient-centered and affordable healthcare. 

When patients have access to tools that make it easier to navigate the healthcare system and connect with care options suited to their needs, they gain more independence and control over their healthcare experience. When doctors and clinical teams have tools that support their back-office and decision-making needs, whether clinical or administrative, they are able to focus on providing care instead of absorbing upkeep tasks or diagnostic uncertainty. When care is delivered proactively rather than reactively, and grounded in clinical evidence rather than consumer trend, outcomes improve before conditions become acute and costly.

5) Investment Strategy 

Our investment strategy centers on identifying high-potential, U.S.-based startups that align with our focus areas and demonstrate strong market potential. We exclusively target Pre-Seed to Seed stage companies, allowing us to invest early in ventures with significant growth potential. Our funding structures typically include preferred equity or convertible notes such as SAFE notes, providing flexibility tailored to early-stage needs. 

In evaluating companies, we prioritize solutions that demonstrate proper market timing and address pressing healthcare challenges with clear product-market fit. We seek ventures offering evidence-based, clinically proven solutions supported by a roadmap for ongoing research and validation. We are particularly drawn to ventures with strong academic incubation, recognition from industry challenges, or early angel funding, all signals of credibility and external validation. 

Regulatory Risk and Revenue Resilience 

Regulatory pathways and clinical validation timelines were the largest blockers we encountered in determining deal validity this past year, particularly at the pre-seed and seed stage, where a company's core technology may be years from clearance while its cash runway is measured in months. We are formalizing a lesson from our own portfolio into a standing diligence requirement: every company whose core product carries meaningful regulatory or clinical-trial risk (e.g., an FDA clearance pathway) must also demonstrate a credible revenue path that does not depend on that pathway clearing on schedule. 

This can take several forms: a services layer that runs alongside the core technology, a data or licensing arm, a staffing or consulting offering, or a lower-risk product tier that does not require the same regulatory clearance. Dopl Technologies is our clearest internal example: while its core ultrasound technology faces a longer regulatory road, the company generates near-term revenue and market validation through a traveling sonographer service, giving it runway and real-world data while the core product pathway advances. Going forward, “path to revenue independent of regulatory timeline” will sit alongside our Five Dimensions of Impact assessment (Section 6) as a required underwriting question, not an optional strength. 

Systems-Level Adoption for Clinical Decision Support and Health Infrastructure 

Clinical Decision Support tools in particular carry a specific trap: it is easy for a founder to validate a tool against a single patient population or a single clinical use case and mistake that validation for market readiness. A tool that performs well in one department, one patient cohort, or one pilot site still has to survive contact with a hospital's actual technology stack, its EHR integration requirements, its credentialing and liability review, and the day-to-day workflow of the physicians and nurses who would need to adopt it. The same is true of Health Infrastructure tools, where the core challenge is rarely whether the software works, but whether it can slot into a health system's existing vendor relationships and procurement process without requiring a rip-and-replace of tools already in place. 

For both segments, we are adding a systems-level adoption requirement to diligence, alongside the clinical or technical evaluation of the product itself. We look for evidence that the founding team is thinking beyond a single successful deployment, specifically: 

Letters of intent, signed pilots, or paid pilot agreements with a health system, rather than informal clinician enthusiasm alone. 

A clear description of how the tool integrates with the systems a hospital already runs (e.g., Epic, Cerner/Oracle Health, existing scheduling or billing infrastructure), rather than an assumption that integration will be straightforward. 

A credible plan for physician and nursing adoption, addressing how the tool changes a clinician's existing workflow rather than adding a parallel one, and who within the health system (department chair, CMIO, nursing leadership) owns the adoption decision. 

Evidence the founder has validated demand across more than one department, site, or patient population, or has an explicit plan to do so, rather than a thesis built entirely on a single successful cohort. 

This does not replace our clinical and technical diligence on the product itself; it sits alongside it. A Clinical Decision Support tool can be clinically excellent and still fail commercially if it was never designed with hospital procurement, IT, and physician workflow in mind, and that systems-level failure mode is different from, and just as important as, the regulatory and reimbursement failure modes already covered in this section. 

A related diligence question for any pilot-stage company: how much of the pilot's success is the founder personally in the room, making introductions, smoothing over integration issues, and keeping the champion engaged, versus the product itself. We ask founders directly whether the pilot would survive and replicate at a new site without them there, and treat a pilot that only works because of the founder's own presence as a key-man risk to flag, not a strength to credit. 

AI Fluency, Evaluation, and Bias Diligence 

AI capability is now the primary driver of both Healthcare Infrastructure and Clinical Decision Support, our two largest and fastest-growing segments. Diligencing these deals well requires the fund itself to build real technical fluency in how these models are built, trained, and validated. That fluency needs to go beyond reading a pitch deck; it means asking specific questions about training data provenance, validation methodology, and failure modes rather than taking a vendor's benchmark claims at face value. We treat developing this fluency as an ongoing exercise for the team, not a one-time competency to check off. 

Within Healthcare Infrastructure, one subsegment we are watching closely is AI evaluation and monitoring infrastructure, tools that assess and continuously monitor the performance of clinical AI and autonomous agents in production, with particular attention to bias and disparate impact across patient subpopulations. This is not a hypothetical category. The FDA's January 2025 draft guidance on AI-enabled device lifecycle management already calls for bias analysis and subgroup performance validation as standard expectations across the full product lifecycle, including after a device reaches market, and the agency separately sought public comment in 2025 on how to measure and manage AI device performance once deployed in the real world.?? As autonomous clinical AI, tools that act with less direct physician oversight, becomes more technically feasible, we expect this evaluation burden to grow, from both regulators and the health systems that will need to satisfy their own liability and equity obligations before deploying these tools at scale. 

We are applying this in two ways. First, as a diligence discipline: any AI-driven Clinical Decision Support or Healthcare Infrastructure company in our pipeline should be able to show how it monitors for bias and performance drift across patient subpopulations, beyond a single aggregate accuracy number, alongside the systems-level adoption criteria above. Second, as a sourcing thesis in its own right: AI evaluation and monitoring tools are themselves an investable subsegment of Healthcare Infrastructure, one we believe is currently underweighted relative to its likely regulatory importance over the next several years. 

Competitive Moat and Defensibility 

AI is collapsing the traditional software moat. A well-designed workflow or clinical interface built on a foundation model is increasingly easy for a well-funded competitor, or the model vendor itself, to replicate in a matter of months rather than years. We are treating defensibility as a standing diligence question rather than an assumption that a good product is its own moat, and we look for durable advantage along one or more of three lines: a hardware or physical-world component that is slow and expensive to replicate, proprietary last-mile data captured through the product's own usage rather than purchased or publicly available data, and a patient or user experience that is difficult to copy because it is built on trust, workflow habit, or clinical relationship rather than feature parity. 

Within Healthcare Infrastructure specifically, the standing question is sharper: does this company's advantage survive a health system's own build-versus-buy decision, and does it survive Epic or another incumbent platform deciding to build the same feature natively. A company whose only moat is a feature that a platform vendor could ship in a point release is not a durable bet. We also underwrite with our actual holding period in mind: our fund expects to hold positions for five to seven years, and the current healthcare venture market is trending toward acquisition rather than IPO as the more likely path to liquidity in that window, a market condition we treat as context for underwriting rather than a fixed assumption about any individual company's outcome. 

Data Governance and Patient Trust 

Every AI-driven company in our pipeline, within Clinical Decision Support, Healthcare Infrastructure, or the diagnostic end of Women's Health, should be able to answer a consistent set of questions about data governance: where its training data came from and whether it was licensed or consented to for this use, how the model's performance is benchmarked on an ongoing basis rather than at launch alone, and what guardrails exist against hallucination or confident-but-wrong outputs in a clinical setting. 

Patient consent and disclosure sit alongside these as a diligence question in their own right. Patients increasingly care whether an AI tool is involved in their care and whether that involvement is disclosed to them, and we treat patient trust as a real clinical and commercial issue, not a public-relations afterthought: a product that cannot clearly explain to a patient what the AI is doing and why they should trust it carries adoption risk that a benchmark accuracy score does not capture. 

Commercial and Clinical Validation Signals 

Beyond the segment-specific criteria above, three signals recur across our strongest evaluations and are worth naming explicitly as things we look for in every deal, regardless of segment: 

A medical champion willing to support and pay. Clinician enthusiasm is common and cheap; a champion willing to advocate for budget, sign a purchase order, or commit departmental resources is rare and far more predictive of real adoption. We weight a paying champion more heavily than a supportive one. 

A credible ability to scale and generate revenue. The product needs to work at one hospital and be structurally repeatable across many, not custom-built per site. We look for a sales motion, pricing model, and integration approach that don't require bespoke re-engineering for every new customer. 

A clinical metric tied directly to impact. The company should be able to name a specific clinical metric it moves, such as readmission rate, time-to-diagnosis, or medication adherence, and show, even at pilot scale, that it actually moves it. A compelling narrative without a named, trackable metric is a yellow flag. 

A credible business model and a named payer. We want to know, specifically, who pays, what they pay, and why they keep paying, whether that is a patient, a provider, a health system, or a payer. At pre-seed and seed, we hold this to a lighter bar than at later stages: a clearly articulated willingness-to-pay hypothesis with early signal (a pilot willing to pay, a stated price a champion has agreed to, a comparable company's pricing) is sufficient; we do not expect a fully proven model this early, but we do expect the question to have been asked and tested rather than deferred. 

One clarification across our Women's Health segment specifically: subcategory labels do not set the evidence bar, product function does. A Women's Health company whose product touches diagnosis, such as the non-invasive and patient-collected diagnostics described in Section 1, is held to the same evidence bar as Clinical Decision Support, not a lighter one, because the underlying risk, a diagnostic claim, is the same regardless of segment. A descriptive or monitoring-only product, by contrast, is evaluated against the Wellness bar. In both cases, the three signals above, a paying champion, scalability, and a named clinical metric, still apply. 

Building the Fund Around Domain Expertise 

We are doubling down on backing founders who are deeply embedded in the healthcare system itself: physicians, nurses, and other healthcare practitioners, healthcare corporate strategists, and health informatics professionals. Deep domain fluency shortens the distance between a clinical insight and a commercializable product, and it materially improves our ability to diligence clinical claims, regulatory pathways, and reimbursement dynamics that a generalist team would need to learn from scratch on every deal. 

That domain fluency extends beyond the founders themselves to who they can reach. A founder embedded in the healthcare system typically carries a network of implementers, department VPs, hospital administrators, and practicing physicians, who can open a pilot or a procurement conversation that a generalist founder would need months to access cold. We weight that network access as part of the domain-expertise thesis, not a separate sourcing advantage. 

We are also prioritizing women founders. Women make up the majority of the healthcare workforce and drive the majority of healthcare purchasing and utilization decisions, yet remain persistently underrepresented in the founder and check-writer seats of healthcare venture funding. Closing that gap is both a return-generating and an impact-generating priority for the fund. 

Across every segment, we favor founders building proactive and preventive solutions with strong clinical grounding over reactive, symptom-management-only products without such grounding. A founder's credibility here is demonstrated through pilot data, published outcomes, clinical advisory involvement, or health system partnerships, not through positioning alone. 

We intend to build this same expertise into the fund itself, not just into our portfolio. Peer healthcare-focused funds have shown that pairing capital with a standing bench of clinical and operating expertise, physicians and nurses who diligence clinical claims, operators who have run commercialization inside health systems, materially improves both deal sourcing and post-investment support. Given our position inside the Yale ecosystem, we are well placed to build that bench by drawing on Yale School of Medicine, Yale School of Nursing, and Yale School of Public Health faculty, alumni, and residents as an ongoing source of clinical diligence partners, advisors, and eventually LPs. 

The best partnerships arise when founders bring energy and excitement about their technology, a diverse team with multiple skill sets enabling adaptability, and a product that fulfills a clear market need. We recognize that our investment size may be smaller compared to traditional VCs, so we prioritize working with founders who see the value in partnering closely with us, leveraging our network of co-investors, healthcare experts, and industry connections to accelerate growth. 

Across segments, we have a stated preference for ventures with a credible path to becoming a longitudinal platform, one that centralizes multiple dimensions of a patient's care over time, rather than a single point-in-time product. A company that starts by solving one problem well and has a plausible roadmap to expand into adjacent touchpoints for the same patient population builds more durable engagement and a larger eventual moat than a narrow tool, and we weight that roadmap, even when it is still early, as a positive signal rather than scope creep. 

6) Impact Thesis and Alignment 

While some argue that healthcare startups are inherently impactful, we believe that companies we invest in should intentionally advance health equity and improve the efficiency, efficacy, and quality of care. We consider this through the lens of the Impact Management Project's Five Dimensions of Impact?: 

1) What is the intended outcome? We assess the startup's target population and condition to determine the significance of its outcomes for those affected. 

2) Who experiences the startup's product? We consider the people and communities experiencing the outcome across characteristics such as gender, class, race, sexual orientation, and Indigenous status, and ask whether the startup focuses on marginalized populations or indications that disproportionately affect them. 

3) How much does a patient or health system pay to access the product, and how many can actually access it? Solutions should maintain or increase access relative to the current standard of care, and costs should not be prohibitive for the target population. 

4) How does the business contribute to the target outcome? We prioritize companies with a “time to impact” of less than seven years, given how long healthcare startups can take to generate clinically adopted results. 

5) What is the risk of the investment? We quantify what happens if the impact does not occur as expected, including whether there are disproportionate downside scenarios beyond negative monetary impact, and, per Section 5, whether a regulatory delay would be fatal absent an alternate revenue path. 

In addition, Rhia Ventures has developed a Health Equity Assessment and Rating Tool (HEART Framework), which scores companies across four categories: (1) Outcomes Oriented, improved outcomes through better diagnostics or therapeutics; (2) Systems Change, systemic change that addresses health equity; (3) Access to Care, enabling access for underserved populations; and (4) Affordability of Care, low or decreased cost of care to the consumer. Each category is scored 1 to 4, and this framework allows us to systematically evaluate startups across the major areas of impact on health equity.

9) Cautionary Tales 

Pear Therapeutics 

Raised: $409M 

Backers: SoftBank, JAZZ Venture Partners, Novartis 

Cause of Death: Un/affordability. Despite being a leader in clinical validation for its FDA-approved app-based therapies for insomnia and substance use disorder, Pear failed to gain widespread adoption because payors denied payment for its clinically effective therapies. The high cost of app access, averaging $1,300 for three months, was a significant barrier compared to traditional prescription medications, ultimately undermining the business model. 

HomeHero 

Raised: $23M 

Backers: Social+Capital, Graham Holdings, TechStars, Science, Cedars-Sinai Accelerator, Tencent, The Launch Fund 

Cause of Death: Regulatory change. A federal ruling forced this senior home care marketplace to reclassify home care workers from independent contractors to employees, significantly increasing costs. The resulting pivot to an enterprise model with large hospital systems struggled with “death by pilot,” as pilot programs failed to convert into sustainable contracts. 

Mindstrong 

Raised: $160M 

Backers: General Catalyst, ARCH Ventures, Optum Ventures, Foresite Capital, 8VC, What If Ventures, Bezos Expeditions 

Cause of Death: Commercial un/viability. This virtual mental health care company faced investor pressure to commercialize prematurely, straining operations. Despite promising low-cost, high-quality care, Mindstrong could not find a profitable business model and was further destabilized by management turnover, ultimately selling its assets to another startup. 

Single-revenue-stream regulatory exposure 

A fourth failure pattern is worth naming explicitly alongside these three: a company whose entire cap table and runway are staked on a single regulatory clearance timeline, with no interim commercial path. This is the pattern our Section 5 revenue-resilience requirement, and the Dopl Technologies example, is designed to guard against going forward. 

10) Yale’s Impact 

This section addresses a different question from the rest of this thesis: not what we invest in, but what we can uniquely bring to it once we have. Yale already runs dedicated pipelines for producing founders, Yale Ventures, Tsai CITY, and CBIT's coursework, hackathons, and pitch nights, but those are inbound programs aimed at Yale students and faculty specifically, and they do not extend to a portfolio company that happens to be founded elsewhere. Our edge is different: the outbound resources we can bring to bear on any portfolio company after we have invested in it, regardless of where it was founded. 

Yale New Haven Health and the Center for Health Care Innovation 

Yale New Haven Health (YNHHS) is Connecticut's largest health system: five hospitals and more than 360 care locations across Connecticut, New York, and Rhode Island, with 2,681 licensed beds and roughly 29,000 employees.? Unlike a loosely affiliated teaching hospital, YNHHS and Yale University jointly govern the Center for Health Care Innovation (CHI), whose stated 2026 priorities, expanding access, workflow redesign, and decentralization of care, map directly onto our own Healthcare Infrastructure and Clinical Decision Support segments.?? This gives our portfolio companies a genuine hospital-scale adoption pathway: LOIs, paid pilots, and integration testing inside a system large enough to constitute real systems-level validation, not a single department's opinion. 

CHI's role extends past adoption into capital. The center describes its mandate as including direct investment in emerging technologies and acting as a limited partner in outside funds.?? That means a YNHHS relationship can plausibly deepen beyond a pilot into co-investment or direct investment in a company we have already backed, a form of downstream capital validation few funds our size can offer. 

Connecticut Innovations 

Connecticut Innovations is the state's quasi-public venture arm and a natural source of follow-on and co-investment capital for our portfolio. Rather than relying on a generic institutional relationship, our access runs through people: relationships built by Yale SOM alumni broadly, and by Meng Fund alumni specifically who have gone on to work in venture and healthcare investing. That gives us a warmer, faster path to a specific person at CI for a specific company, rather than a cold introduction. 

The Graduate Student Bench 

Yale's graduate population is a diligence and staffing asset distinct from the physician and practitioner talent thesis in Section 5. Where Section 5 describes who we prioritize backing and recruiting onto our standing bench, this is about the deep, rotating pool of expertise the fund can pull from for any specific deal without hiring for it: PhD researchers in biomedical engineering, public health, and data science who can pressure-test a clinical or technical claim; MBA students with healthcare operating or finance backgrounds who can stress-test a go-to-market model or unit economics; and joint-degree students who sit across both. This lets us staff a diligence team matched to what a specific deal actually needs, rather than relying on generalist judgment alone. 

Why This Depends on Section 5 

None of these three resources, YNHHS and CHI, Connecticut Innovations, and Yale's graduate talent pool, convert into value automatically. Each depends on the fund's own relationships and the domain-expertise bench described in Section 5: a physician or researcher who can broker a YNHHS pilot, an alum who can make a warm introduction at CI, a fund lead who knows which graduate program to pull a reviewer from. Yale's institutional scale is only as useful as our ability to activate it, which is why Section 5's talent thesis is not a separate initiative from this one, but the mechanism that makes it work.
